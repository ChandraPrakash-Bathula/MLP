Abstract

We follow one patient through a five-layer multilayer perceptron and derive every quantity the network computes: the standardised inputs, each affine map and activation, the variance argument behind He and Xavier initialization, batch normalization and its backward pass, dropout as an implicit ensemble, the cross-entropy loss from the Bernoulli likelihood, backpropagation as reverse-mode differentiation, the stability condition for gradient descent, and the optimizers that cope with ill-conditioning, saddle points and plateaus. Each derivation is paired with a figure computed from the actual network, so every number on the page can be traced from first principles.

Part I sets the stage for practitioners: where neural networks came from, how an artificial neuron relates to a biological one and to logistic regression, why hidden layers are needed, and what the whole pipeline looks like in an applied project. After every key equation, a teal In plain words box restates the idea without mathematics and ties it to a real use case.

How to read this page. The bar at the top chooses one of 61 held-out test patients, the activation and the initialization. Figures marked live recompute for that patient; run marks a real training run; toy marks a two-parameter illustration.

Part I
Foundations

Eighty years of ideas in five short sections, adapted from the Deep Learning series on Towards AI, Parts 1–4.

AA brief history of deep learning

Nearly every component on this page is decades old. What changed after 2010 was the amount of data, the speed of GPUs, and a handful of training techniques that made deep networks trainable.

Why it matters

Each breakthrough fixed a specific failure of the one before. The perceptron could not learn XOR (section C), so hidden layers were needed. Hidden layers could not be trained until backpropagation (section 7). Deep stacks trained poorly until ReLU, careful initialization, batch norm and dropout (sections 3–5). Knowing the failure each idea solves is the fastest way to know when you need it.

In plain words

The recipe for deep learning was written long ago; the kitchen arrived late. A network that learns from examples needs many examples and a lot of arithmetic. Until roughly 2010 there was neither enough labelled data nor fast enough hardware, so simpler methods won. ImageNet (1.2 million labelled photos) and gaming GPUs changed that, and AlexNet's 2012 win made the whole field switch almost overnight.

AppliedThe same economics apply in your organisation. If a problem has thousands of rows and a few dozen columns, classical models are often enough. If it has millions of rows, or raw images, text or audio, deep learning tends to win, because performance keeps improving as data grows while classical methods level off.

Further reading: Deep Learning Series, Part 1: a brief history of deep learning.

BBiological neurons and artificial neurons

An artificial neuron borrows one idea from biology: a cell that adds up weighted incoming signals and "fires" when the total is large enough.

Definition · McCulloch–Pitts neuron (1943) and Hebb's rule (1949)
\[ y = \mathbb{1}\Bigl[\, \sum_{i} w_i x_i \ge \theta \,\Bigr], \qquad x_i, y \in \{0, 1\} \tag{B.1} \] \[ \Delta w_i = \eta\, x_i\, y \qquad \text{("cells that fire together wire together")} \tag{B.2} \]

McCulloch and Pitts showed that networks of such threshold units can compute any logical function, but their weights were set by hand. Hebb proposed that a connection strengthens whenever the two cells it joins are active together, the first rule for learning weights from experience.

In plain words

A neuron is a tiny voting booth. Each input casts a vote, the weight says how much that vote counts, and if the weighted total clears a bar the neuron says "yes". Learning means changing how much each vote counts. It works the way expertise does: a radiologist who has read thousands of scans has strengthened exactly the connections that matter, so the right pattern is recognised in seconds.

AppliedThe analogy stops there. Your brain has about 86 billion neurons and 1014 synapses; the network on this page has 41 neurons and 573 weights. Artificial networks are inspired by biology but are really just well-organised arithmetic, which is why they can be trained, inspected and deployed like any other software.

CThe perceptron and logistic regression

Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) was the first neuron that learned its own weights. Replace its hard step with a smooth sigmoid and you get logistic regression, which is exactly the output unit of our network.

Definition · two learning rules with the same shape
\[ \text{Perceptron:}\quad \hat y = \mathbb{1}[\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b \ge 0], \qquad \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} + \eta\, (y - \hat y)\, \mathbf{x} \tag{C.1} \] \[ \text{Logistic regression:}\quad \hat y = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b), \qquad \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\, (\hat y - y)\, \mathbf{x} \tag{C.2} \]

The updates look identical, but the perceptron only moves when it makes a mistake (\(y - \hat y \in \{-1, 0, 1\}\)), while logistic regression moves on every example in proportion to how wrong its probability is. The second rule is gradient descent on the cross-entropy loss, which section 6 derives as eq. (6.3).

Proof · no single neuron computes XOR

Suppose \(\mathbb{1}[w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b \ge 0]\) computes XOR. The four rows of its truth table require

\[ b \lt 0, \qquad w_1 + b \ge 0, \qquad w_2 + b \ge 0, \qquad w_1 + w_2 + b \lt 0 \tag{C.3} \]

Adding the middle two gives \(w_1 + w_2 + 2b \ge 0\), so \(w_1 + w_2 + b \ge -b \gt 0\), contradicting the last inequality. The same holds for a sigmoid unit thresholded at 0.5.

In plain words

A perceptron is a rubber stamp: approved or rejected, with no sense of how sure it is. Logistic regression is the same machine with a dial: "73% likely". The dial matters because a probability can be ranked, thresholded later, and trained smoothly. But both can only draw one straight line through the data, and some patterns, such as "exactly one of these two things is true", cannot be split by any single line.

AppliedAlways fit logistic regression first. It is fast, explainable, and is the baseline any neural network must beat. On these 242 training patients it reaches … on the 61 test patients; compare the MLP in section 11. If the MLP cannot clearly beat it, ship the simpler model.

DWhy hidden layers: the multilayer perceptron

Stack neurons in layers and let the hidden layer invent new features. Two hidden units are enough to solve XOR, and the same idea, scaled up, is the network studied in Part II.

Construction · XOR from two hidden units
\[ \begin{aligned} h_1 &= \mathbb{1}[x_1 + x_2 - 0.5 \ge 0] &&(\text{OR}) \\ h_2 &= \mathbb{1}[x_1 + x_2 - 1.5 \ge 0] &&(\text{AND}) \\ \hat y &= \mathbb{1}[h_1 - h_2 - 0.5 \ge 0] &&(\text{XOR}) \end{aligned} \tag{D.1} \]

The output computes "OR but not AND", which is XOR. Each unit on its own is a perceptron; the combination is not.

In plain words

A hidden layer is a set of intermediate questions the network asks itself. For XOR they are "is at least one input on?" and "are both on?". Given those answers, the final decision is easy. Deep learning works the same way at scale: early layers learn simple signals, later layers combine them into more useful ones, and the last layer only has to draw a straight line in that new space. Figure 11.3 shows our trained network doing exactly this with real patients.

AppliedYou never design these intermediate questions by hand. That is the main practical difference from classical machine learning, where an analyst engineers features such as "income-to-debt ratio". An MLP learns its own features from the raw columns, given enough data.

Training a network is like a student working through a practice book. 1The learning problem

Supervised learning chooses parameters that make a model's predictions agree with labelled examples, in the hope that the agreement carries over to patients the model has never seen.

Why it matters

Everything later in this article (the architecture, the loss, the optimizer) is a design choice in service of one objective. Stating that objective precisely tells us what "learning" means and what can go wrong: a model can drive the objective down on the training data while getting worse on new data.

Definition · empirical risk minimization

Given \(N\) training pairs \(\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}\) and a model \(f_{\boldsymbol\theta}\), training solves

\[ \boldsymbol\theta^\star = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\; \hat R(\boldsymbol\theta) = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal{L}\bigl(f_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf{x}_i),\, y_i\bigr) \tag{1.1} \]

What we actually care about is the expected risk \(R(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)}[\mathcal{L}(f_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf{x}), y)]\). The test set estimates it, and the difference

\[ \text{generalization gap} = R(\boldsymbol\theta) - \hat R(\boldsymbol\theta) \tag{1.2} \]

is what regularization (sections 4, 5 and 10) tries to keep small. Here \(N = 242\) training patients and 61 test patients, split once with a fixed seed.

In plain words

Training is studying with past exam papers that come with an answer key. Eq. (1.1) says: adjust the model until its answers on those papers are as good as possible. The test set is the real exam with new questions. The generalization gap (1.2) is the difference between memorising the answer key and actually understanding the subject.

AppliedA churn model is trained on last year's customers but judged on how well it flags next quarter's. Always keep data the model never sees during training, and when the data has a time order, split by time rather than at random.

1.1Standardisation and conditioning

Each feature is converted to a z-score using statistics of the training set only:

\[ x_j = \frac{\text{raw}_j - \mu_j}{\sigma_j}, \qquad \mu_j = \frac1N \sum_i \text{raw}_{ij}, \quad \sigma_j^2 = \frac1N\sum_i (\text{raw}_{ij} - \mu_j)^2 \tag{1.3} \]

Data leakage. Computing \(\mu, \sigma\) on all 303 patients would let information from the test set shape the training inputs, and the test score would be optimistic.

Derivation · why scale affects learning

For a linear model with squared loss, \(\hat R(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2N}\lVert X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\rVert^2\), the Hessian is constant:

\[ H = \tfrac1N X^{\top} X, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\lambda_{\max}(H)}{\lambda_{\min}(H)} \tag{1.4} \]

Gradient descent with the best fixed step shrinks the error by a factor \(\tfrac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}\) per step. Cholesterol (\(\sigma \approx 51\)) next to sex (\(\sigma \approx 0.47\)) puts eigenvalues roughly \(10^4\) apart, so \(\kappa\) is huge and progress along the small directions crawls. Standardising equalises the diagonal of \(H\) and typically shrinks \(\kappa\) by orders of magnitude. The same intuition carries over to neural networks, whose first layer is exactly such a linear map.

In plain words

Standardising puts every column in the same units: "how unusual is this value for a patient?". Without it, cholesterol (values in the hundreds) would shout over sex (0 or 1) simply because its numbers are bigger, and training would zigzag slowly, like walking down a long narrow canyon instead of a round bowl.

AppliedFit the scaler on training data only and save it with the model. In production, every new loan application or transaction must be scaled with the same stored means and standard deviations, or the model sees numbers it was never trained on. A scikit-learn Pipeline does this automatically.

In practice
  • Fit the scaler on the training split only, then apply the same \(\mu, \sigma\) to validation and test data.
  • cp and thal are categorical. We standardise their codes to keep the example small; one-hot encoding is the more principled choice.

2Architecture

A multilayer perceptron is a composition of simple maps: an affine transformation followed by an element-wise non-linearity, repeated layer after layer.

Why it matters

Composition is what gives depth its power. Each layer re-represents its input, so later layers can build features out of earlier ones. The architecture fixes the hypothesis class: which functions the network can express at all, before any training happens.

Definition · layer and network
\[ \mathbf{z}^{(l)} = W^{(l)} \mathbf{a}^{(l-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(l)}, \qquad \mathbf{a}^{(l)} = \phi\bigl(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\bigr), \qquad l = 1, \dots, 4 \tag{2.1} \] \[ \hat y = f_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf{x}) = \sigma\Bigl(W^{(5)}\, \phi\bigl(W^{(4)} \cdots \phi(W^{(1)} \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)}) \cdots\bigr) + b^{(5)}\Bigr) \tag{2.2} \]

For a mini-batch stacked as rows of \(A^{(l-1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{|B| \times n_{l-1}}\), the same computation is one matrix product per layer, which is why GPUs train networks quickly:

\[ A^{(l)} = \phi\bigl(A^{(l-1)} W^{(l)\top} + \mathbf{1}\, \mathbf{b}^{(l)\top}\bigr) \tag{2.3} \] \[ |\boldsymbol\theta| = \sum_{l=1}^{5} \bigl(n_{l-1} n_l + n_l\bigr) = 224 + 204 + 104 + 36 + 5 = 573 \tag{2.4} \]
In plain words

Think of the network as a chain of analyst teams. The first team of 16 reads the 13 raw measurements and each writes its own score; the next team of 12 reads those 16 scores, and so on, until a single analyst gives the final probability. Every arrow between two analysts is one weight, a dial saying how much one listens to the other. This network has 573 such dials, and training means finding good settings for all of them.

AppliedFor tabular use cases, 2–4 hidden layers of 16–256 units are typical. Keep an eye on the ratio of parameters to training rows: 573 parameters for 242 patients is a lot, which is why sections 4, 5 and 10 are needed.

Theorem · universal approximation (Cybenko 1989; Hornik 1991; Leshno et al. 1993)

Let \(\phi\) be continuous and not a polynomial, \(K \subset \mathbb{R}^d\) compact, \(g : K \to \mathbb{R}\) continuous and \(\varepsilon > 0\). Then there exist \(m\), \(\mathbf{w}_j\), \(b_j\), \(v_j\) such that

\[ \sup_{\mathbf{x} \in K}\; \Bigl|\, g(\mathbf{x}) - \sum_{j=1}^{m} v_j\, \phi(\mathbf{w}_j^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b_j) \Bigr| \lt \varepsilon \tag{2.5} \]

The theorem guarantees existence, not learnability, and \(m\) may need to be enormous. Depth helps in practice because some functions computed by deep networks need exponentially many units to match with a single hidden layer (Telgarsky, 2016).

In plain words

With enough neurons, a network can imitate any smooth pattern, the way enough Lego bricks can build any shape. But the theorem only says the right bricks exist. It does not say how many you need, whether training will find them, or whether you have enough data to tell a real pattern from noise.

3The neuron, its initialization and its activation

Each unit computes an inner product, adds a bias and applies a non-linearity. How its weights start, and which non-linearity it uses, decide whether signals and gradients survive five layers.

3.1The affine map

Definition
\[ z = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x} + b = \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{in}}} w_i x_i + b, \qquad a = \phi(z) \tag{3.1} \]

Geometrically, \(z = 0\) is a hyperplane in input space with normal \(\mathbf{w}\); \(z\) is a signed, scaled distance from it. A ReLU unit is "on" on one side of its hyperplane and zero on the other, so a ReLU network partitions input space into polyhedral regions and is linear within each one.

In plain words

One neuron works like a loan officer with a scorecard: each factor (income, debt, history) is multiplied by how much it matters, the results are added to a baseline score (the bias), and the total \(z\) is turned into a decision by the activation. 3.2Initialization: fan-in, fan-out and the variance argument

Why it matters

Training starts from the initial weights. If their scale is wrong, each layer multiplies the signal's variance by a constant factor, so after five layers activations either vanish or explode, and so do gradients. If all weights are equal, every unit in a layer computes the same thing and receives the same gradient, so the units can never become different (the symmetry problem).

Derivation · He and Xavier initialization

Assume weights are i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance \(\sigma_w^2\), independent of the inputs. The fan-in \(n_{\text{in}}\) is the number of inputs to a unit; the fan-out \(n_{\text{out}}\) is the number of units it feeds. From (3.1),

\[ \operatorname{Var}(z) = \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{in}}} \operatorname{Var}(w_i x_i) = n_{\text{in}}\, \sigma_w^2\, \mathbb{E}[x^2] \tag{3.2} \]

For ReLU with \(z\) symmetric about zero, half the mass is cut off, so \(\mathbb{E}[a^2] = \tfrac12 \operatorname{Var}(z)\). Requiring the second moment to be preserved from layer to layer, \(\mathbb{E}[a^{(l)2}] = \mathbb{E}[a^{(l-1)2}]\), gives

\[ \tfrac12\, n_{\text{in}}\, \sigma_w^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \sigma_w^2 = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}} \qquad \text{(He et al., 2015)} \tag{3.3} \]

The same argument applied to the backward pass, \(\operatorname{Var}(\delta^{(l)}) = n_{\text{out}}\, \sigma_w^2\, \mathbb{E}[\phi'^2]\, \operatorname{Var}(\delta^{(l+1)})\), asks for \(\sigma_w^2 \propto 1/n_{\text{out}}\). For activations that are roughly linear near zero (tanh, sigmoid), Glorot and Bengio (2010) compromise between the two:

\[ \sigma_w^2 = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}} \tag{3.4} \]
In plain words

Initialization sets the volume knob of each layer before learning starts. Fan-in is how many voices speak into one neuron. If each voice is loud, their sum is deafening; if each is a whisper, the sum is silence, and after five layers the message is lost, like a game of telephone. He initialization sets the volume to \(\sqrt{2/\text{fan-in}}\) so the signal arrives at the last layer at the same loudness it started. The 2 compensates for ReLU silencing about half the neurons. And the weights must be random: if all start equal, all neurons learn the same thing.

AppliedPyTorch and Keras already initialise sensibly, so you rarely set this by hand. Check it when a model's loss does not move at all from the first epoch, or when you build custom layers.

3.3Activation functions

Why it matters

Without a non-linearity, depth adds nothing: composing affine maps gives another affine map,

\[ W^{(2)}\bigl(W^{(1)}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{(1)}\bigr) + \mathbf{b}^{(2)} = \underbrace{W^{(2)}W^{(1)}}_{W}\,\mathbf{x} + \underbrace{W^{(2)}\mathbf{b}^{(1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(2)}}_{\mathbf{b}} \tag{3.5} \]

so a 5-layer linear network is just logistic regression. The activation's derivative also matters, because backpropagation multiplies by \(\phi'(z)\) at every layer (section 7).

Definitions and derivatives
\[ \text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z), \quad \text{ReLU}'(z) = \mathbb{1}[z \gt 0]; \qquad \text{Leaky}(z) = \max(\alpha z, z),\; \alpha = 0.1 \tag{3.6} \] \[ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \quad \sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr) \le \tfrac14; \qquad \tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^2(z) \le 1 \tag{3.7} \]

Sigmoid and tanh saturate: for large \(|z|\) their derivatives are nearly zero. ReLU has derivative exactly 1 when active, which keeps gradients alive, but a unit whose \(z\) is negative for every input never updates again (a "dead" ReLU). Leaky ReLU keeps a small slope to prevent this.

In plain words

The activation is the neuron's decision rule. ReLU says "if the evidence is negative, stay quiet; if positive, pass it on". This small bend is what lets stacked layers learn curved boundaries; without it, five layers collapse into one big linear regression (eq. 3.5). Sigmoid squashes any number into a probability between 0 and 1, which is perfect for the final answer but slows learning inside the network because it flattens out at both ends.

AppliedReLU (or GELU) in hidden layers. For the output: sigmoid for yes/no (disease, fraud, churn), softmax for one-of-\(K\) (product category), and no activation for numbers (price, demand).

4Batch normalization

Batch normalization standardises each unit's pre-activations over the mini-batch, then lets the network learn the scale and shift it actually wants.

Why it matters

Ioffe and Szegedy (2015) introduced it to reduce "internal covariate shift": the distribution of a layer's inputs changes as earlier layers train. Later analyses attribute most of its benefit to a smoother, better-conditioned loss surface (Santurkar et al., 2018) and to the scale invariance in eq. (4.5), which together allow larger learning rates and make training less sensitive to initialization.

Definition · training mode

For one unit over a mini-batch \(z_1, \dots, z_m\):

\[ \mu_B = \frac1m \sum_{k=1}^{m} z_k, \qquad \sigma_B^2 = \frac1m \sum_{k=1}^{m} (z_k - \mu_B)^2 \tag{4.1} \] \[ \hat z_k = \frac{z_k - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon}}, \qquad y_k = \gamma\, \hat z_k + \beta \tag{4.2} \]

At inference a single patient has no batch, so running averages collected during training replace the batch statistics (PyTorch uses \(\rho = 0.9\)):

\[ \bar\mu \leftarrow \rho\, \bar\mu + (1 - \rho)\, \mu_B, \qquad \bar\sigma^2 \leftarrow \rho\, \bar\sigma^2 + (1 - \rho)\, \sigma_B^2 \tag{4.3} \]
Derivation · backward pass and scale invariance

Every \(z_k\) affects every \(\hat z_j\) through \(\mu_B\) and \(\sigma_B\). Writing \(g_k = \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial y_k\), the chain rule gives

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_k} = \frac{\gamma}{m\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon}} \Bigl( m\, g_k - \sum_{j} g_j - \hat z_k \sum_{j} g_j\, \hat z_j \Bigr), \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \gamma} = \sum_k g_k \hat z_k, \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \beta} = \sum_k g_k \tag{4.4} \]

The gradient is centred and decorrelated from \(\hat z\): it carries no component that would merely shift or rescale the batch. And because normalisation removes scale,

\[ \text{BN}\bigl(\alpha W \mathbf{a}\bigr) = \text{BN}\bigl(W \mathbf{a}\bigr) \;\;\forall \alpha \gt 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla_{\alpha W}\, \mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{\alpha}\, \nabla_{W} \mathcal{L} \tag{4.5} \]

so larger weights automatically receive smaller gradients, a built-in stabiliser. The engine on this page implements (4.4) and was checked against finite differences.

In plain words

Batch norm is grading on a curve. For each neuron, it takes the scores of the current batch of patients and rescales them to average 0 and spread 1, so the next layer always receives inputs in a familiar range, however much the earlier layers have changed. The learned \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\) then let the network choose its own curve if centred scores are not ideal.

AppliedBatch norm lets deeper networks train faster and with larger learning rates. Its most common bug: forgetting model.eval() before predicting, so a single customer's prediction gets normalised by statistics of whatever batch it happens to be in.

In practice
  • Call model.eval() before validation so that eq. (4.3) is used; forgetting it is a common source of "great training, poor test" results.
  • Batch statistics are noisy for small batches (below about 16). Layer normalization, which normalises across units of one example, is used in Transformers and recurrent networks instead.
  • The bias \(\mathbf{b}^{(l)}\) of a layer followed by batch norm is redundant, since \(\beta\) absorbs it.

5Dropout

During training, each hidden unit is removed at random with probability \(p\). Every step therefore trains a different thinned sub-network, all sharing the same weights.

Why it matters

With 573 parameters and 242 patients, the network can memorise. Dropout prevents units from co-adapting, each one relying on specific partners, and it approximates averaging an ensemble of \(2^{40}\) thinned networks (one per subset of the 40 hidden units) at the cost of training one (Srivastava et al., 2014).

Definition · inverted dropout
\[ m_j \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1 - p), \qquad \tilde a_j = \frac{m_j\, a_j}{1 - p}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\tilde a_j] = a_j \tag{5.1} \]

Scaling the survivors by \(1/(1-p)\) during training keeps every expectation unchanged, so at test time the full network is used as is.

Derivation · dropout as data-dependent regularization

For a linear unit fed by dropped-out inputs, \(z = \sum_i w_i \tilde a_i\), the mean is unchanged but noise is injected:

\[ \mathbb{E}[z] = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{a}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(z) = \frac{p}{1 - p} \sum_i w_i^2\, a_i^2 \tag{5.2} \]

The expected loss therefore gains a penalty that grows with \(w_i^2 a_i^2\): an L2 penalty weighted by how strongly each input is used (Wager, Wang and Liang, 2013). Keeping dropout on at test time and averaging \(T\) passes gives a Monte Carlo estimate of the prediction and its uncertainty (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016):

\[ \hat y_{\text{MC}} = \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^{T} f\bigl(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{m}^{(t)}\bigr), \qquad \hat s^2 = \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^{T} \bigl(f(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{m}^{(t)}) - \hat y_{\text{MC}}\bigr)^2 \tag{5.3} \]
In plain words

Dropout is a team that practises with random players sitting out. No one can lean on a single star teammate, so everyone learns to contribute, and the team plays better on match day when all players are present. Running the network many times with different players out (eq. 5.3) is like asking 300 slightly different experts: if they agree, trust the answer; if they disagree, the case is uncertain.

AppliedIn medical triage or fraud review, route the cases where the 300 predictions spread widely to a human reviewer. This is a cheap way to add an "I'm not sure" signal to an existing network.

In practice

\(p\) between 0.1 and 0.5 for hidden layers; call model.eval() to switch dropout off. Combining dropout and batch norm in the same block can shift the variance between training and test (Li et al., 2019); placing dropout after the activation, as here, and using modest \(p\) keeps the effect small.

6Likelihood and loss

The output unit returns \(\hat y = \sigma(z^{(5)})\), read as \(P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})\). The loss follows from asking which parameters make the observed labels most probable.

Why it matters

The loss is the only thing the optimizer sees. A loss derived from the likelihood gives calibrated probabilities and, combined with the sigmoid, a gradient that is large exactly when the model is confidently wrong.

Derivation · binary cross-entropy from the Bernoulli likelihood
\[ P(y \mid \mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol\theta) = \hat y^{\,y}\, (1 - \hat y)^{1 - y} \tag{6.1} \] \[ \mathcal{L}(\hat y, y) = -\log P(y \mid \mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol\theta) = -\bigl[\, y \log \hat y + (1 - y) \log(1 - \hat y) \,\bigr] \tag{6.2} \]

Minimising the average of (6.2) over the training set is maximum-likelihood estimation. Its derivative with respect to the logit \(z = z^{(5)}\) simplifies remarkably:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z} = \underbrace{\Bigl(-\frac{y}{\hat y} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - \hat y}\Bigr)}_{\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \hat y}\; \underbrace{\hat y\, (1 - \hat y)}_{\sigma'(z)} = \hat y - y \tag{6.3} \]
Why not mean squared error?

With \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 (\hat y - y)^2\) and a sigmoid output, the factor \(\sigma'(z)\) no longer cancels:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}}{\partial z} = (\hat y - y)\, \hat y\, (1 - \hat y) \tag{6.4} \]

If the model is confidently wrong (\(\hat y \to 0\) while \(y = 1\)), this gradient goes to zero and learning stalls exactly when it is needed most. Eq. (6.3) stays at \(-1\).

In plain words

The loss is the grade the model gets for each answer. Cross-entropy is a strict teacher: answering "10% chance of disease" for a sick patient costs a lot, and "1%" costs far more. It punishes confident mistakes hardest, like a forecaster who said 99% sunny on the day of a storm. Its gradient, \(\hat y - y\), is simply "how far off was the probability", and that number starts the whole backward pass.

AppliedMatch the loss to the output: cross-entropy for classification, mean squared or absolute error for numbers. For rare positives (fraud, rare disease), add a class weight such as pos_weight so missing a positive costs more than a false alarm.

Convexity, locally

With the hidden layers frozen, \(\hat y = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^{(5)\top}\mathbf{a}^{(4)} + b^{(5)})\) is logistic regression on the learned features \(\mathbf{a}^{(4)}\), and its training loss is convex in \(\mathbf{w}^{(5)}\). Figures 6.2 and 6.3 slice exactly this surface. The full loss, as a function of all 573 parameters, is not convex.

In practice

Compute the loss from the logit, not from \(\hat y\): BCEWithLogitsLoss evaluates \(\mathcal{L} = \max(z, 0) - z y + \log(1 + e^{-|z|})\), which never takes the log of a number that has underflowed to zero.

7Backpropagation

Backpropagation computes \(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \theta\) for all 573 parameters in one backward sweep, by applying the chain rule from the loss towards the inputs and reusing intermediate results.

Why it matters

Estimating each derivative by finite differences would cost one extra forward pass per parameter, 573 passes per step here and billions for large models. Reverse-mode differentiation obtains all of them for a small constant multiple of one forward pass (the "cheap gradient principle"; Linnainmaa 1970, Baur and Strassen 1983). That cost difference is what makes training deep networks feasible.

7.1Local derivatives on a computational graph

Every computation is a graph of primitive operations, each with a simple local derivative. Multiplication swaps its inputs; addition passes the gradient through unchanged; the chain rule multiplies local derivatives along each path:

\[ f = x\,y:\;\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = y,\;\; \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = x \qquad\quad q = x + y:\;\; \frac{\partial q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial q}{\partial y} = 1 \qquad\quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial q}\,\frac{\partial q}{\partial x} \tag{7.1} \]
In plain words

Every complicated calculation is built from small steps, and each step knows its own simple rule for passing blame backwards. For multiplication, the blame for one input equals the other input: if revenue is price × quantity, selling one extra unit adds exactly one price to revenue. For addition, the blame is passed through unchanged. The chain rule multiplies these small rules along the path.

7.2The backpropagation equations

Definition · error signals and gradients

Let \(\boldsymbol\delta^{(l)} = \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\). Using \(\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l+1)}/\partial \mathbf{a}^{(l)} = W^{(l+1)}\) and \(\partial \mathbf{a}^{(l)}/\partial \mathbf{z}^{(l)} = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\phi'(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})\bigr)\):

\[ \delta^{(5)} = \hat y - y \tag{7.2} \] \[ \boldsymbol\delta^{(l)} = \bigl(W^{(l+1)\top}\, \boldsymbol\delta^{(l+1)}\bigr) \odot \phi'\bigl(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\bigr), \qquad l = 4, \dots, 1 \tag{7.3} \] \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W^{(l)}} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(l)}\, \mathbf{a}^{(l-1)\top}, \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{b}^{(l)}} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(l)} \tag{7.4} \]

For a mini-batch, the gradients (7.4) are averaged over its examples. Each layer reuses \(\mathbf{a}^{(l-1)}\) and \(\mathbf{z}^{(l)}\) cached during the forward pass.

Vanishing and exploding gradients

Unrolling (7.3) from the output to layer 1,

\[ \boldsymbol\delta^{(1)} = \Bigl(\prod_{l=1}^{4} \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\phi'(\mathbf{z}^{(l)})\bigr)\, W^{(l+1)\top}\Bigr)\, \delta^{(5)}, \qquad \bigl\lVert \boldsymbol\delta^{(1)} \bigr\rVert \le \prod_{l=1}^{4} \Bigl(\max|\phi'| \cdot \lVert W^{(l+1)} \rVert\Bigr)\, |\delta^{(5)}| \tag{7.5} \]

A product of many factors smaller than one vanishes; larger than one, it explodes. With sigmoid, \(\max|\phi'| = \tfrac14\), so each layer can shrink the error signal fourfold. ReLU (\(\phi' \in \{0, 1\}\)) together with He initialization keeps the factors near one.

In plain words

Backpropagation is a post-mortem memo travelling back through the organisation chart. The output says "we were off by \(\hat y - y\)". Each layer forwards the blame to the people who reported to it, in proportion to how much it relied on them (the weights) and only if it was actually listening (\(\phi' \neq 0\)). Each weight's gradient is then its share of the blame times the input it received. If the memo is diluted at every level, the first layers never hear it: that is the vanishing gradient.

AppliedIf early layers of a deep model stop learning, use ReLU, He initialization and batch norm, as here; very deep models add residual (skip) connections. Frameworks compute all of this with one call, loss.backward().

8Gradient descent and the step size

The gradient gives the direction of steepest increase; gradient descent steps the other way. The step size \(\eta\) decides whether that step helps.

Definition
\[ \boldsymbol\theta_{t+1} = \boldsymbol\theta_t - \eta\, \nabla \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta_t) \tag{8.1} \]
Derivation · when does a step decrease the loss?

Write \(\mathbf{g} = \nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta)\). A second-order Taylor expansion along the step gives

\[ \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta - \eta\,\mathbf{g}) \approx \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta) - \eta\, \mathbf{g}^{\top}\mathbf{g} + \frac{\eta^2}{2}\, \mathbf{g}^{\top} H\, \mathbf{g} \tag{8.2} \]

The first-order term always helps; the curvature term hurts if \(\eta\) is too large. The right side is minimised by, and decreases only for,

\[ \eta^\star = \frac{\mathbf{g}^{\top}\mathbf{g}}{\mathbf{g}^{\top} H\, \mathbf{g}}, \qquad 0 \lt \eta \lt 2\eta^\star \tag{8.3} \]

On a quadratic loss, the iteration is stable in every direction only if \(\eta \lt 2/\lambda_{\max}(H)\). The sharpest direction sets the speed limit for all the others, which is why ill-conditioning (large \(\kappa\), eq. 1.4) makes plain gradient descent slow.

In plain words

Gradient descent is a hiker in thick fog trying to reach the valley floor. They cannot see the valley, only feel the slope under their feet, so they take a step downhill and feel again. The learning rate \(\eta\) is the stride length: too short and the hike takes forever; too long and they leap across the valley and end up higher on the other side. Drag the slider in Figure 8.1 past \(2\eta^\star\) to see the loss go up.

AppliedThe learning rate is the most important setting to tune. Start from the default (0.001 for Adam), and if training is unstable divide by 3; if it is slow, multiply by 3. A learning-rate schedule that lowers it over time usually helps at the end.

9Optimizers

Real loss surfaces are ill-conditioned, flat in places and full of saddle points, and real gradients are noisy estimates. Each optimizer below fixes one of these problems.

9.1Stochastic gradients and batch size

Definition
\[ \hat{\mathbf{g}} = \frac{1}{|B|} \sum_{k \in B} \nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\, \mathcal{L}_k, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{g}}] = \nabla \hat R, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\mathbf{g}}) \approx \frac{\Sigma}{|B|} \tag{9.1} \]

A mini-batch gradient is unbiased, and its noise shrinks as \(1/|B|\). Batch gradient descent uses all \(N\) examples (one exact step per epoch); SGD uses one (\(N\) noisy steps); mini-batches trade between the two and fill a GPU efficiently. Moderate noise also helps escape saddle points (Ge et al., 2015; Jin et al., 2017).

In plain words

Batch gradient descent polls the whole country before every decision: accurate but slow. SGD asks one random person: fast but noisy. A mini-batch surveys 32 people: a good estimate at a fraction of the cost. The noise is not all bad; it jostles the model out of flat spots where an exact method would stall.

AppliedBatch sizes of 32–256 are standard for tabular data. Larger batches use GPUs better but often need a larger learning rate to match.

9.2Momentum and Nesterov

Definition · heavy ball (Polyak, 1964) and Nesterov (1983)
\[ \mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \beta\, \mathbf{v}_t + \mathbf{g}_t, \qquad \boldsymbol\theta_{t+1} = \boldsymbol\theta_t - \eta\, \mathbf{v}_{t+1} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \sum_{k=0}^{t} \beta^{\,t-k}\, \mathbf{g}_k \tag{9.2} \]

The velocity is an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients. Components that keep their sign accumulate, with effective step \(\eta/(1-\beta)\) (ten times larger at \(\beta = 0.9\)); components that alternate cancel. Nesterov's variant evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point \(\boldsymbol\theta_t - \eta\beta\mathbf{v}_t\). On a quadratic with condition number \(\kappa\), tuned gradient descent and heavy-ball momentum contract the error per step by

\[ \rho_{\text{GD}} = \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}, \qquad \rho_{\text{momentum}} = \frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1} \tag{9.3} \]

so for \(\kappa = 100\), momentum needs about ten times fewer steps.

In plain words

Momentum turns the hiker into a heavy ball. A ball rolling down a valley builds up speed in the consistent downhill direction and smooths out the side-to-side wobble, and its speed carries it over small bumps and through flat stretches where a hiker would stop. \(\beta = 0.9\) means the ball remembers roughly its last ten steps.

9.3Adaptive methods: RMSprop and Adam

Definition · per-coordinate step sizes
\[ \mathbf{s}_t = \beta_2\, \mathbf{s}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, \mathbf{g}_t^{2}, \qquad \boldsymbol\theta_{t+1} = \boldsymbol\theta_t - \frac{\eta\, \mathbf{g}_t}{\sqrt{\mathbf{s}_t} + \epsilon} \qquad \text{(RMSprop)} \tag{9.4} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{m}_t &= \beta_1 \mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, \mathbf{g}_t, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{m}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{m}_t}{1 - \beta_1^{\,t}}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{s}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{s}_t}{1 - \beta_2^{\,t}} \\ \boldsymbol\theta_{t+1} &= \boldsymbol\theta_t - \eta\, \frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{s}}_t} + \epsilon} \qquad \text{(Adam)} \end{aligned} \tag{9.5} \]
Derivation · Adam's bias correction

With \(\mathbf{m}_0 = \mathbf{0}\), unrolling the recursion and assuming stationary gradients,

\[ \mathbf{m}_t = (1 - \beta_1) \sum_{k=1}^{t} \beta_1^{\,t-k}\, \mathbf{g}_k \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{m}_t] = (1 - \beta_1) \frac{1 - \beta_1^{\,t}}{1 - \beta_1}\, \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}] = \bigl(1 - \beta_1^{\,t}\bigr)\, \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}] \tag{9.6} \]

The running mean is biased toward zero early on, so it is divided by \(1 - \beta_1^{\,t}\) (Kingma and Ba, 2015). On the first step \(\hat{\mathbf{m}}_1 = \mathbf{g}_1\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{s}}_1 = \mathbf{g}_1^2\), so every coordinate moves by about \(\eta\,\operatorname{sign}(g)\), regardless of how small the gradient is. That is why adaptive methods walk off plateaus (Figure 9.3).

In plain words

Adam gives every one of the 573 weights its own step size. Weights whose gradients are consistently tiny get their steps enlarged; weights with large, jumpy gradients get them reduced. It is momentum plus automatic per-weight volume control, which is why it works well out of the box on most problems with little tuning.

AppliedAdam (or AdamW, when you also use weight decay) with learning rate 0.001 is the default choice in industry. Switch to SGD with momentum only when you have time to tune and evidence it generalises better on your data.

9.4Critical points: minima, saddles and plateaus

Definition · classification by the Hessian
\[ \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta + \Delta) \approx \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol\theta) + \nabla\mathcal{L}^{\top}\Delta + \tfrac12\, \Delta^{\top} H\, \Delta, \qquad H = Q\, \Lambda\, Q^{\top} \tag{9.7} \]

At a critical point (\(\nabla\mathcal{L} = 0\)) the eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) of \(H\) are the curvatures along its eigenvectors: all positive is a local minimum, all negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle point, and all near zero a plateau. In high dimensions, most critical points with high loss are saddles rather than poor minima (Dauphin et al., 2014).

Why plain gradient descent is slow at a saddle

Near a saddle, along an eigenvector with \(\lambda \lt 0\), eq. (8.1) gives \(\Delta_{t+1} = (1 + \eta|\lambda|)\, \Delta_t\):

\[ \Delta_t = (1 + \eta\, |\lambda|)^{t}\, \Delta_0 \tag{9.8} \]

Escape is exponential, but if the start lies almost exactly on the ridge (\(\Delta_0 \approx 0\)), many steps pass before it begins. Momentum accumulates the small consistent push; adaptive methods rescale it to size \(\eta\); gradient noise kicks the iterate off the ridge.

In plain words

A saddle point is a mountain pass: downhill in one direction, uphill in the other, and almost flat at the very middle, so a careful hiker standing there feels no slope and barely moves. A plateau is a flat desert where every direction looks the same. Large networks have many more saddles and plateaus than bad valleys, so the real danger is getting stuck on flat ground, not in the wrong valley. Pick "Saddle point" or "Plateau" in Figure 9.3 and press Race to watch plain SGD stall while Adam walks away.

AppliedIf a training curve sits flat for many epochs and then suddenly drops, the model was crossing a saddle or plateau. Do not stop training at the first flat stretch; use early stopping on validation loss with some patience instead.

In practice
  • Adam or AdamW with \(\eta = 10^{-3}\) is a robust default; all three of this week's notebooks use Adam.
  • For convolutional networks trained for a long time, SGD with momentum 0.9 and a decaying schedule often generalises slightly better.
  • AdamW applies weight decay as \(\boldsymbol\theta \leftarrow \boldsymbol\theta - \eta\lambda\boldsymbol\theta\) outside the adaptive scaling (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019), which plain Adam with an L2 term does not.

10Training and regularization

With 573 parameters and 242 patients, the network can fit the training set almost perfectly. Whether it generalises is an empirical question, so we answer it with a controlled experiment.

Why it matters

Low training loss is not the goal; low expected risk, eq. (1.2), is. Regularization trades a little training fit for a smaller generalization gap. The classic forms are an explicit penalty and early stopping,

\[ \min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\; \hat R(\boldsymbol\theta) + \lambda \lVert \boldsymbol\theta \rVert_2^2, \qquad t^\star = \arg\min_{t}\; \hat R_{\text{val}}(\boldsymbol\theta_t) \tag{10.1} \]

and batch norm and dropout act as implicit regularizers through their noise.

Experimental design

Four networks are trained side by side with the same initialization seed, data order, optimizer and learning rate, so the only difference is the regularizer: plain, batch norm, dropout \(p = 0.3\), and both. Training loss is evaluated with dropout off so the curves are comparable.

In plain words

Four students study the same book. The first memorises every answer: perfect on practice papers, shaky on the real exam. The others use study habits (batch norm, dropout) that slow memorising and force real understanding. Figure 10.1 shows the exam results epoch by epoch. When the plain network's training loss keeps falling while its test loss rises, you are watching it memorise.

AppliedAlways plot training and validation loss together. Stop training (early stopping) when validation loss stops improving, and save the weights from that epoch rather than the last one.

11Evaluation and learned representations

A probability model is judged on held-out data, at a threshold and across all thresholds, and by what its hidden layers have learned to represent.

Definitions
\[ \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \qquad F_1 = \frac{2\,\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}} \tag{11.1} \]

The ROC curve traces the true-positive rate against the false-positive rate as the threshold sweeps from 1 to 0. The area under it has a probabilistic meaning: it is the chance that a random patient with disease is scored higher than a random patient without,

\[ \text{AUC} = P\bigl(\hat y(\mathbf{x}^+) \gt \hat y(\mathbf{x}^-)\bigr) \tag{11.2} \]

To look at the learned representation we project each test patient's last hidden activations \(\mathbf{a}^{(4)} \in \mathbb{R}^4\) onto the leading eigenvectors of their covariance (principal component analysis):

\[ C = \frac1n \sum_i \bigl(\mathbf{a}_i - \bar{\mathbf{a}}\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf{a}_i - \bar{\mathbf{a}}\bigr)^{\top} = U \Lambda U^{\top}, \qquad \mathbf{p}_i = U_{:,1:k}^{\top}\bigl(\mathbf{a}_i - \bar{\mathbf{a}}\bigr) \tag{11.3} \]
In plain words

Precision answers "of the patients we flagged, how many were actually sick?". Recall answers "of the sick patients, how many did we catch?". AUC asks how well the model ranks people: pick one sick and one healthy patient at random; AUC is the chance the sick one gets the higher score. The PCA plots look inside the network: the last hidden layer has learned a 4-number summary of each patient in which sick and healthy patients sit apart.

AppliedChoose the decision threshold by the cost of each mistake, not by default at 0.5. In screening, a missed patient is expensive, so lower the threshold to raise recall. In spam or fraud alerts that go to customers, false alarms annoy people, so favour precision. Then monitor these numbers after deployment, because data drifts.

11.2Summary

  1. Training minimises empirical risk (1.1); we care about the expected risk, estimated on held-out data.
  2. Standardising inputs improves conditioning (1.4); He and Xavier initialization preserve signal variance across layers (3.3)–(3.4).
  3. Non-linear activations make depth meaningful (3.5); their derivatives decide whether gradients survive (7.5).
  4. Cross-entropy is the negative Bernoulli log-likelihood, and its gradient at the logit is \(\hat y - y\) (6.3).
  5. Backpropagation obtains every gradient in one backward sweep (7.2)–(7.4).
  6. The step size is limited by curvature (8.3); momentum and adaptive methods address ill-conditioning, saddles and plateaus (9.2)–(9.8).
  7. Batch norm and dropout regularise and stabilise training; their effect is measured, not assumed (Figure 10.1).
In plain words

Scale the data, pass it forward through layers of weighted sums and simple bends, score the answer with a loss, send the blame backwards with the chain rule, nudge every weight a little downhill, and repeat for many mini-batches. Batch norm and dropout keep this stable and honest; an optimizer like Adam picks sensible step sizes. Judge the result only on data the model has never seen, choose the threshold by what each kind of mistake costs, and keep checking it after deployment.

AppliedThe same eight steps, with a different input layer and output layer, are how teams build credit-risk, churn, fraud and demand models. Convolutional networks and Transformers change the layers in step 3, but steps 1–2 and 4–8 stay the same.
